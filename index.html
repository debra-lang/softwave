About Find My Quiet Sound — a free tinnitus sound generator for masking, relaxation and sleep

Find My Quiet Sound plays steady, seamless sounds that can make ringing, buzzing or hissing in the ears less noticeable for some people. Choose from white, pink and brown noise, gentle static, rain, rain on a window, ocean waves, lapping water, flowing water, a waterfall, forest, rustling leaves, crickets, cicadas, a summer night, wind, a fan, a fireplace and night sounds. Every sound is generated live in your browser, so nothing loops with a click and nothing needs to download.

What it does

Layer up to five sounds in the mixer, explore a frequency generator, try guided tinnitus sound matching, set a sleep timer with gradual fade-out, and watch calm visuals while you listen.

Who it is for

People with tinnitus who want an easy, free way to add a comfortable background sound — at a desk, in a quiet room or at bedtime. It works with speakers, headphones or a pillow speaker, on phones, tablets and computers.

How to start

Tap a sound, keep the level low — just enough to blend with the tinnitus — and try others until one feels comfortable. There is no single sound that works for everyone.

Octave check

Why this step: tones an octave apart (double or half the frequency) sound alike in character, so self-matching often lands exactly one octave off. Listen to all three at the same low level and keep the one that feels closest. This is an estimate for your own exploration, not a clinical measurement.

Listen well

A few simple principles from tinnitus and hearing-health organisations.

Safe listening

  • Start low. Use the lowest comfortable listening level that gives you the experience you want. You do not need to completely cover your tinnitus. Louder is not necessarily better. Find My Quiet Sound starts every sound low and fades it in.
  • Take breaks. Pause during longer listening sessions. If a sound causes discomfort or seems to make your tinnitus worse, stop using it and lower the volume before trying again.
  • Headphone time. The WHO-ITU safe-listening standard suggests keeping personal audio around 80 dB or below for no more than 40 hours a week (75 dB for children). A device’s volume percentage does not reliably map to decibels, so the practical rule stays the lowest comfortable level.
  • Speakers are fine. A speaker or a pillow speaker at night can be just as useful as headphones.
  • Sound sensitivity (hyperacusis). Stay at the quietest setting, and seek a clinician’s plan if everyday sounds hurt.

When to talk to a professional

Sound tools can help day to day, but please see a doctor, audiologist or ENT (ear, nose and throat) specialist if your tinnitus:

  • is new, or changed suddenly
  • is persistent, is getting worse, or is bothering you significantly
  • is only in one ear
  • comes with hearing difficulty, fullness in the ear, or dizziness
  • pulses with your heartbeat
  • started after an injury, or is affecting sleep, mood or concentration

A hearing evaluation is the usual first step. Options such as hearing aids, sound-based management and counselling approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy are available, and professionals can help you choose.

How sound can help

Tinnitus often feels loudest in silence. Adding a pleasant, steady sound can make it less noticeable, give your attention something else to rest on, and may help with relaxation and sleep. Individual experiences vary — it is a way of managing the experience, not a treatment.

There is no single best sound. Broadband noise (white, pink, brown) and natural sounds are the most widely used; what matters is what feels comfortable to you.

Privacy

Find My Quiet Sound works without an account. Your favourites, presets and settings are stored only on this device. Nothing about your hearing is collected or sent anywhere, and the optional "Find My Tinnitus Sound" result is saved only if you choose to.
